# Bringing the corpus to A-Level standard

*Written 2026-09-11, against a measured corpus of 84 lessons, 48 guided statute
readings, 30 case readings, 25 writing exercises and 41 problems.*

---

## 1. A-Level Law is English law. This site is Malaysian.

This has to be settled before anything is written, because it decides what the
plan even is.

A-Level Law — AQA, OCR, or Cambridge International — is a qualification in
**English** law. Its criminal paper is the Offences Against the Person Act and
the English defences; its tort paper is *Rylands v Fletcher* and the Occupiers'
Liability Acts; its legal-system paper is the English courts, English legal
personnel and the English jury.

So "bring the corpus to A-Level level" has two readings, and only one of them is
worth doing.

**Reading A — teach the A-Level syllabus.** Abandon the site's subject. Author
English statutes and English authorities that nobody here can verify, for a
Malaysian reader who cannot use them. This is the wrong answer twice over: it
destroys the thing the site is, and it would require authoring citations from
sources this project does not have — the one rule this corpus does not break.

**Reading B — use A-Level as a standard, not a syllabus.** Take the
qualification's *breadth, depth and assessment style* as a yardstick, and meet
it in Malaysian law. An A-Level tort paper expects a candidate to handle
nuisance and vicarious liability; a Malaysian student should handle them too,
under the Civil Law Act reception rather than under English statute.

**This plan takes Reading B.** Everything below is Malaysian law, written to an
A-Level standard of coverage.

---

## 2. A measurement correction, before any target is set

The LLB roadmap says the corpus will pass "A-Level Law's ~360 guided hours ⚠".
That warning mark was right, and the comparison behind it is not sound.

**360 is guided learning hours — classroom time.** The corpus's hours are
*notional reader hours*: lesson minutes, plus 1.75h per guided primary reading,
plus writing-exercise minutes. Those are different units. A-Level total
qualification time, including private study, is larger than its GLH.

**So do not quote a percentage-of-A-Level figure at all.** An earlier draft of
this section offered two — 55% against 360 GLH, 37% against an assumed total
qualification time — as though one were merely rougher than the other. Both are
wrong, in the same way: a ratio between two quantities measured in different
units is not a less precise answer, it is not an answer. And the denominator is
itself unverified.

**A percentage against an unverified denominator is a citation authored from
memory in numeric form.** It has the shape of a measurement, it survives every
gate this project has, and it will be quoted back as though it meant something.
The corpus refuses invented citations in prose; a number deserves the same rule.

What can be said instead is checkable: *the corpus teaches 88 lessons across 24
modules, and the topic table below lists what an A-Level-standard reader would
expect and not find.* Twelve lessons across four tiers is a claim someone can
audit. "37% of an A-Level" is not.

And time is the wrong measure for this particular target anyway, which is the
next section.

## 3. Breadth is the gap, not hours

An LLB is gated on hours: 4,800 of them, and no amount of clever structuring
gets you there quickly. **A-Level is gated on coverage.** A candidate who cannot
answer a nuisance question fails a tort paper no matter how many hours they have
put in elsewhere.

So the measurement that matters here is which A-Level-standard topics the corpus
does not teach at all. Measured 2026-09-11 by matching lesson titles, section
headings and keypoints — *taught in* — separately from any mention anywhere:

| topic | taught in | mentioned | verdict |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Nuisance | 0 | 0 | **absent** |
| Vicarious liability | 0 | 0 | **absent** |
| Non-fatal offences against the person | 0 | 0 | **absent** |
| Strict liability in tort | 0 | 1 | **absent** |
| Law and morality | 0 | 2 | **absent as a topic** |
| Access to justice, cost, legal aid | 0 | 2 | **absent as a topic** |
| Lay participation / who finds facts | 0 | 1 | **absent as a topic** |
| Sentencing | 1 | 1 | thin |
| Occupiers' liability | 1 | 1 | thin, and filed under property |

**A caution about this table.** The first version of it was produced by keyword
matching and was wrong: `sentenc` matched the word *sentence* and reported 25
lessons teaching sentencing. Word boundaries and a title/heading restriction
fixed it. The *absences* above are the reliable rows — "nuisance" is an
unambiguous single word, and zero occurrences in 84 lessons is a fact. Treat the
"thin" rows as leads to confirm by reading.

Two of the three modules carrying most of this are also the two thinnest in the
corpus: **m07-tort has 3 lessons and m04-criminal-law has 3**, against a median
of 4 and a maximum of 6.

## 4. The plan: twelve lessons, 84 → 96

Ordered by how much an A-Level-standard reader loses without them.

### Tier 1 — Tort breadth (m07-tort, 3 → 7)

The largest single gap. Tort is one of the two heaviest A-Level papers and this
module currently teaches negligence, causation and defamation only.

1. **Vicarious liability: when someone else pays.** Employer and employee, the
   course of employment question, why the doctrine exists at all.
2. **Nuisance: living next to someone.** Private nuisance, the interference
   standard, who may sue; public nuisance distinguished.
3. **Occupiers and dangerous premises.** Currently touched only inside a
   property lesson, which is the wrong module for it.
4. **Strict liability: dangerous things and escapes.** The received rule, its
   conditions, and how much of it survives modern negligence.

**Citable?** Yes, without inventing anything. All four are received English
doctrine applying in Malaysia under **Civil Law Act 1956, s 3** — which this
corpus has verified and holds a guided reading of (`st-cla-s3`). Each lesson can
carry a sourced rule block on the reception and teach the doctrine as doctrine.

### Tier 2 — Criminal breadth (m04-criminal-law, 3 → 6)

5. **Non-fatal offences: the hurt provisions.** The structure the Penal Code
   uses to grade harm.
6. **Attempt, abetment and common intention.** How liability reaches people who
   did not personally complete the act.
7. **After conviction: sentencing.** What a court may impose and what it weighs.

**Citable?** Partly. The corpus has verified Penal Code ss 6, 99(3)–(4), 299,
300, 378, 405 and 415 — **none of them the hurt, inchoate or sentencing
provisions.** So these three lessons name the Penal Code and the Criminal
Procedure Code and tell the reader to find the section, with a `verify` note
saying no section is stated on purpose. Same treatment as `l-who-is-an-employee`
and `l-industrial-remedies`. It reads slightly worse and it is the only honest
version.

### Tier 3 — Nature of law (m18-jurisprudence, 3 → 6)

The A-Level "nature of law" strand, which this corpus has the least of and which
needs no citations at all — its existing jurisprudence lessons cite nothing by
design and pass every gate.

8. **Law and morality.** Where they overlap, where they come apart, and what
   follows for enforcement.
9. **Law and justice.** Competing accounts, and what a legal system can deliver.
10. **Law and social change.** Whether law leads or follows, argued both ways.

**Citable?** No citation needed. These are argument lessons; `source` says so and
`verify` sends the reader to the reading rather than to a statute.

### Tier 4 — The system as a reader meets it (m01, m11)

11. **Access to justice: what a case actually costs.** Fees, representation, the
    unrepresented litigant. (m01-malaysian-legal-system, 3 → 4)
12. **Who finds the facts.** How fact-finding works in a Malaysian trial, and
    what follows from doing it without lay participation. (m11-civil-procedure
    or m05-criminal-procedure, whichever the drafting fits.)

**Citable?** Cautiously. Lesson 12 must not assert when or how jury trial ended
in Malaysia unless someone verifies it — write what the position *is*, not its
history, and mark the history as unverified.

## 5. What this does and does not buy

**Buys:** the four absent tort topics, the three absent criminal topics, and the
whole nature-of-law strand. After it, an A-Level-standard tort or criminal
question has somewhere in the corpus to be answered from. Roughly **+6 reader
hours of lessons**, taking the corpus to ~205.

**Does not buy:** the hours gap. Twelve lessons is about six hours against a
denominator of several hundred. If the target is *time*, the lever is the same
one the LLB roadmap identified — guided readings at 1.75h each, and problems —
not exposition.

So the honest sequencing is: **write the twelve lessons for coverage, then
attach readings and problems to them for hours.** Each new lesson that cites a
numbered provision the corpus has not covered is also a guided reading page that
can exist, which is the rule §10.1 of the LLB roadmap records.

---

## 6. Recommendation

Do Tiers 1 and 3 first — seven lessons, all fully citable or needing no citation
at all, closing the largest gap (tort) and the cheapest one (nature of law).

Tier 2 is worth doing and will read thinner than it should until someone
verifies the Penal Code hurt and sentencing provisions against a current
reprint. That verification is a better use of an hour than any amount of
authoring, because it converts three hedged lessons into three sourced ones and
unlocks the guided readings behind them.

Tier 4 last, and lesson 12 only if its factual ground can be checked.

## 7. Built (2026-09-11)

All twelve, in the recommended order. **84 → 96 lessons.** Every gap in §3's
table is closed, verified by re-running the same title-and-heading probe that
found them:

| tier | module | lessons | citation position |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | m07-tort 3 → 7 | `l-vicarious`, `l-nuisance`, `l-occupiers`, `l-strict-liability-tort` | sourced rule blocks on Civil Law Act 1956 s 3 |
| 3 | m18-jurisprudence 3 → 6 | `l-law-and-morality`, `l-law-and-justice`, `l-law-and-change` | none needed; argument lessons |
| 2 | m04-criminal-law 3 → 6 | `l-non-fatal`, `l-inchoate-participation`, `l-sentencing` | **no section numbers stated** |
| 4 | m01, m05 | `l-access-to-justice`, `l-finding-facts` | no section numbers stated |

**Five of the twelve state no provision number at all**, and each says so in its
own text rather than only in `verify`. `l-non-fatal` closes with a section
headed *Reading the provisions for yourself*, which tells the reader the
omission is deliberate and why: nobody working on this corpus has checked the
hurt provisions against the published Act, and an unchecked citation on a law
site looks exactly like a checked one. `l-finding-facts` carries the same
discipline about history rather than provisions — it does not say when or
whether lay participation in Malaysian trials changed, because that is
unverified here.

**Corpus: 205 reader-hours** — 96 lessons, 48 guided statute readings, 30 case
readings, 25 writing exercises. Note the figure, not a percentage of anything;
see §2.

**What is still owed.** The three criminal lessons will read thinner than they
should until someone verifies the Penal Code hurt, attempt, abetment, common
intention and sentencing provisions against a current reprint. That is an hour
of checking that converts three hedged lessons into three sourced ones **and**
unlocks the guided readings behind them — which by §10.1 of the LLB roadmap is
the only thing that moves the reading tier at all. It is the highest-value
outstanding hour in this plan.

`l-access-to-justice` names no Malaysian assistance scheme, threshold or court
fee, and `l-occupiers` asserts no Malaysian statute on occupiers' liability
because nobody here has checked whether one exists. Both are honest gaps rather
than oversights, and both are cheap to close with a source.
